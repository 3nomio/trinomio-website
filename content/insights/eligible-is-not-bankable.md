## The paradox

Costa Rica produces almost all of its electricity from renewable sources. Its banks are liquid. It has a national taxonomy of sustainable finance that tells lenders and investors, in a common language, what counts as green. And yet much of the country's industry still raises steam and hot water by burning fuel oil and diesel, and green lending to industry remains a small fraction of what the opportunity allows.

If the money exists and the clean electricity exists, why don't the two meet? The usual answer is that projects are too risky or returns too low. In my experience, that is rarely the real reason. The obstacle is not the absence of capital. It is the absence of a path that turns an engineering opportunity into an asset that a credit committee can approve and an investor can hold.

This article proposes a simple way to see that path. I call it a funnel of three steps: **value discovery, value separation and value transfer**. Costa Rica is good at the first and increasingly ready for the third. What it lacks is the middle step.

## Three barriers that are not about money

Before describing the funnel, it helps to name what blocks it.

The first barrier is **knowledge**. A credit analyst is trained to read balance sheets, not heat balances. When a plant proposes to replace a fuel-oil boiler with a heat pump, the analyst sees a capital expense and an unfamiliar technology. The savings are real, but they are written in kilowatt-hours and coefficients of performance, not in cash flows and coverage ratios. What cannot be read cannot be priced, and what cannot be priced is declined or left waiting.

The second barrier is **transaction cost**. Many industrial energy projects are small, a few hundred thousand dollars each. Assessing a small loan takes almost as much effort as assessing a large one, so the margin disappears in the analysis. Banks naturally prefer fewer, larger deals.

The third barrier is **unassessed environmental and social risk**. Retiring old equipment leaves behind fuel tanks, possible soil contamination and disposal questions. New equipment brings its own questions, such as refrigerants and worker safety during installation. When no one has mapped these risks, a prudent lender assumes the worst.

None of these barriers is solved by more liquidity. Each is solved by information, standardisation and structure.

## The funnel: four filters, four disciplines

A project becomes financeable only after it passes four filters, in order, and each belongs to a different discipline.

- **1. Is it eligible?** This is a question of physics: does the new system really use less energy than the one it replaces, measured on a fair and agreed basis?
- **2. Is it bankable?** This is a question of finance: at the plant's real electricity tariff, do the savings produce enough cash to repay a loan?
- **3. Is it acceptable?** This is a question of environmental and social standards: are the risks of removing the old equipment and installing the new one identified and managed?
- **4. Is it green under the taxonomy?** This is a regulatory question: does the activity meet the national criteria that allow it to be labelled and reported as sustainable?

Most projects that fail do so not because they are bad, but because they were tested against only one or two of these filters, by people from only one or two of these disciplines.

## Value discovery: eligible is not bankable

The first step of the funnel is to find where value exists. Here lies the most common misunderstanding in green finance: the assumption that a project which saves energy will also save money.

It is not always so. An electric boiler can be more efficient than a fuel-oil boiler and still cost more to run, because a kilowatt-hour of electricity may be more expensive than the equivalent heat from cheap fuel. A heat pump, which delivers several units of heat for each unit of electricity, changes the arithmetic completely, especially if it runs at the hours when electricity is cheapest.

The tool that connects engineering to credit is the **parity price**: the highest electricity price at which switching technologies still pays. Below that price the project is bankable; above it, it is not. Parity turns a technical debate into a financial threshold that any analyst can understand and any lawyer can write into a covenant.

Discovery, then, means two things: confirming the physical saving, and confirming that the saving survives contact with the tariff.

## Value separation: the missing middle

Discovered value is not yet an asset. It is a promise spread across a plant's operations, mixed with its other costs and risks. To finance it, someone has to **separate** it: isolate the cash flow that the new equipment creates, describe its risks, and present it in a form a lender can assess on its own merits.

This is the step Costa Rica is missing. Plants do not do it, because it is not their business. Banks do not do it, because they lack the technical knowledge. Equipment suppliers could do it, but they have an obvious interest in the sale. What is needed is a translator in the middle: a firm that turns physics into cash flows, and cash flows into a **complete case file**, covering the client, the asset, the expected savings, the risks and the structuring options.

Separation also means **segmenting risk**, so that each lender takes only the part it understands:

- large plants can borrow directly, on the strength of their balance sheets;
- mid-sized plants can be financed through their equipment suppliers;
- small firms can be grouped, so that many small projects are assessed as one portfolio, supported by development-banking guarantees and by cooperatives that already know their clients.

Where a plant is unwilling to put its own money into the project, separation allows the equity to come from someone else: a leasing company, an energy-service company that sells heat rather than equipment, or a structured vehicle that pools several projects. The point is not which of these prevails. The point is that once value is separated and standardised, any of them can finance it.

## Value transfer: from loans to the capital market

The final step is to move the value to whoever can hold it best.

At first, that holder is a bank. But a bank's appetite has limits. Prudential rules cap how much it can lend to a single sector, client or group, and capital requirements tie up resources with every loan. An energy transition of national scale would quickly push lenders against those ceilings, and would compete with every other sector that also needs green investment.

The way out is to **recycle** the portfolio. Once a bank holds many loans, each with a standardised and measured saving, it can group them and refinance them through a green bond or a regulated investment fund, selling them to investors in Costa Rica or abroad. The capital returns to the bank, which can lend again. A modest first stage can then grow many times over without the bank carrying all of it.

This only works if the earlier steps were done well. Investors in a green bond want three things: clear eligibility criteria aligned with the taxonomy, tracking of every loan financed, and credible reporting of the impact, in kilowatt-hours saved, in money and in tonnes of CO₂ avoided. A portfolio built through a disciplined funnel already contains all three. A portfolio assembled loan by loan, without common standards, contains none.

## What it will take

The funnel of discovery, separation and transfer is not a theory. It describes what any green asset must go through before capital can reach it. Costa Rica has unusual advantages at both ends: clean electricity to discover value, and a capital market and taxonomy ready to transfer it. The weak link is the middle.

Strengthening it is a task for the readers of this article:

- **for banks**, to build credit teams and products that can read energy projects, and to segment risk rather than refuse it;
- **for lawyers**, to design contracts, guarantees and vehicles that isolate the value of energy savings and make it transferable;
- **for regulators and market institutions**, to keep standards common and simple, so that portfolios built today can be refinanced tomorrow.

> Green finance is a funnel of discovery, separation and transfer. Costa Rica already has the beginning and the end. It is time to build the middle.
